"It's all a lie, the baddest bitches ain't in LA." Adéla wrote that line about Bratislava — about growing up sharing a bedroom with a sibling, in a small country most of her American audience couldn't point to on a map, and deciding that didn't disqualify her from anything. She wasn't thinking about the Philippines when she wrote it. But borrow the line, point it at Quezon City instead of Slovakia, and it holds up perfectly: the baddest bitches were never just in LA. A lot of them are right here.

"Ain't in LA" is, in Adéla's own words, an anthem for immigrants, transplants, and dreamers from small towns far from Hollywood's spotlight. That's not a stretch to apply here — it's basically the Filipino experience in one sentence. But this piece isn't really about the song. It's about who gets to be called "baddest" in this country, and who society still tries to write out of that sentence entirely.

Every Kind of Woman, Carrying Something Heavy

Filipino women are, statistically and culturally, expected to carry a lot. The market vendor who's up before sunrise. The nurse who left her own kids with a lola so she could raise someone else's country's healthcare system. The single mother stitching together three incomes. The provincial breadwinner sending remittances to a family she hasn't hugged in years. Filipino culture loves to praise the "ilaw ng tahanan" — the light of the home — right up until that same woman asks for equal pay, bodily autonomy, or a say in decisions made about her.

And then there's a whole category of Filipina womanhood that doesn't even get the "ilaw ng tahanan" praise to soften the exclusion: transgender women. Filipino culture has a long, complicated relationship with gender nonconformity — a cultural visibility that goes back generations, folded into words like "bakla," alongside a legal system that still refuses to recognize a trans woman's identity in anything close to the way it recognizes anyone else's.

A Law Older Than Some of the People Who Need It

The SOGIE Equality Bill was first filed in the Philippine Congress in 2000. It has been refiled, revised, and reintroduced in nearly every Congress since, including a fresh version — House Bill No. 5266 — filed in October 2025 by Representatives Brian Poe, Ryan Recto, and Javier Miguel Benitez. It proposes fines between ₱100,000 and ₱500,000 and one to twelve years imprisonment for SOGIESC-based discrimination in workplaces, schools, and public spaces. It has passed the House before. It has never survived the Senate.

In the meantime, the discrimination the bill is meant to address doesn't pause and wait for legislation. Trans Filipinas are routinely denied services, misgendered on official documents, harassed in public spaces, and shut out of job opportunities for the simple reason of being visibly, unapologetically themselves. The bill isn't asking for special treatment. It's asking for the floor everyone else already stands on.

Protect the Dolls

In February 2025, designer Conner Ives closed his London Fashion Week show wearing a plain white T-shirt reading "Protect the Dolls" — made the night before with heat-transfer paper, worn on impulse. "Dolls" is ballroom-scene slang, dating back to the 1980s, affectionately used for trans women and trans femmes. The shirt went viral within days. Pedro Pascal, Troye Sivan, Addison Rae, and dozens of others wore it in public. Sales have since raised over $600,000 for Trans Lifeline and Not a Phase, two organizations that directly support trans people. In 2026, MAC Cosmetics partnered with Ives on a lipstick-and-T-shirt collection for International Transgender Day of Visibility, continuing the same message.

"I was raised and supported by incredible and wonderful women. I wouldn't be here now if it weren't for the 'dolls.'" — Conner Ives

That sentiment translates directly here. Protecting the dolls in a Philippine context means protecting the trans women who've been part of Filipino community life for generations — often celebrated as entertainers, stylists, and pageant queens, but rarely protected as citizens with full legal standing. Visibility without protection isn't the same as acceptance. It's applause with an asterisk.

Every kind of woman belongs in that phrase — the OFW mother, the barangay health worker, the trans woman fighting for a law that still doesn't fully see her, the teenager figuring out who she is in a country that's still figuring out how to make room for her. None of them need Los Angeles, or anyone else's approval, to be exactly what Adéla was singing about. The baddest bitches were never confined to one zip code. They're in Quezon City, in Iloilo, in Davao, in every barangay where a woman is out here surviving and thriving on her own terms, loudly, unapologetically, and without waiting for permission.
